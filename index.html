ACT ONE — THE GIFT

♪ 1. A Morning in Hialeah

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — opening ensemble: Hialeah's world, Hugh's hustle, and its power structure.

MUSICAL NUMBER — one big opening ensemble number weaving together Hialeah's world, Hugh's hustle, and its power structure. 12-year-old Hugh, 1984. Establishes Hialeah's warmth in full color — bright, lived-in single-family homes, community spaces full of life, chalk art on concrete, murals on every surface the block can claim. No cycle of erasure yet — that fight comes later. The threat instead sits quieter, at the edges: the racetrack looming bright over the block while the streets below stay dim, numbers runners ducking between shops, a rooster-emblem sedan idling too long outside a storefront, "insurance" collectors making their rounds like it's just another Tuesday. Mom's "I'm proof your hard work matters — but remember to slow down, enjoy this life too" line carries the urgency of her own arrival on the Freedom Flights, over a decade behind her now. Hugh's promise to do great things. His jobs plant Willy at the marina and the shared charter-boat dream, and how thin the line is between the hustle and outright theft. But his art — the drawing, the painting, the shed — stays private, an escape he doesn't share. A glimpse of the Roosters and Raccoons doing something quietly shady on the edge of the number — and Nina and Vera catching it, trading a look, already starting to track their movements, the neighborhood's unofficial eyes and ears. Roosters and Raccoons established as the neighborhood's street-level power. Music DNA: 60% Los Van Van (relentless, funk-tinged current) for the hustle and threat underneath, 30% Tres Coronas (bright, driving son cubano) for community warmth, 10% Celia Cruz & Willie Colón horns cutting through for defiance. Listening: Los Van Van, "Apaga La Luz" (Celia & Willie).

2. Ray's Shop

Ray's early warmth toward Hugh — and something a little off, unexplained yet.

Ray Cash's warmth toward Hugh planted early — personal, not just principled. Mid-conversation, he catches sight of the Roosters walking by outside the window, goes tense and watchful, tracking them until they're out of sight — then eases right back into his warm self like nothing happened. Hugh barely registers it.

3. 3 Juan's Pizza

Hugh gets a free slice; fatherly warmth from the counter — and overhears talk of the bad dream spreading shop to shop.

Hugh gets a free slice on his way home. Fatherly advice from behind the counter, no charge — another small, quiet source of warmth for a kid without one at home. Regulars trade worried talk over the register: another shop owner on the block had that same dream, woke up in a cold sweat certain something terrible was coming — and by the end of the week, was paying the Roosters for "protection" just to sleep at night.

4. Inciting Incident

Junior's arrested; Dario begs Hugh for bail money — a real, wrenching choice, since it's the money he'd saved for his mom's window AC unit. He gives it up anyway.

Junior arrested — the easy money finally catches up with him. Dario begs Hugh for bail money through the window. It's not an easy yes — that money was earmarked for his mom, a window AC unit so she wouldn't have to suffer through another Miami summer, and Hugh hesitates, really wrestles with it, before he hands it over anyway. His dream, let go for a friend.

♪ 5. The Painted World

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — Hugh's I Want song, a reaction to the day. Prince's grandmother reveals both boys' gifts at once, and names the difference between them.

MUSICAL NUMBER — Hugh's "I Want" song: wonder and longing as the gift shows itself for the first time, a melody "The Fire" will later reprise and break, and "The Gift Reignites" will finally answer. Still shaken from the day — Junior, the money, the ache of giving it up — Hugh retreats to his art shed, alone, and that's when it happens: the shed transports him into the idealized Hialeah, wondrous and entirely his. He comes back out changed, and runs into Prince — already a friend — who invites him home, where Prince's grandmother is making a traditional Haitian treat. It's over the food, in her kitchen, that she names both boys' gifts at once. She's the one who recognizes what Hugh has: a keeper of her family's Haitian spiritual tradition, generations deep. In the same breath, she finally lets Prince in on his own — small hand-shaped figures, sealed with intent, a pwen, a point of focus used to carry protection and healing toward someone who needs it, the family's craft passed down at last. What Prince has, she tells him, was taught, carried down, earned through lineage. What Hugh has wasn't taught to anyone — it's just love doing what the current always does when it's real. Something old and sad crosses her face when she says it doesn't always stay sweet. Neither boy understands what she means. Neither will, for years.

6. First Brush with Magic

A painted bird plants the park idea in the mayor's sleep.

A bird Hugh painted lifts off the canvas and flies to the mayor's window that night, whispering the park idea into his sleep. PLOT POINT ONE: the gift's first real ripple into the world.

ACT TWO A — CONSEQUENCES

7. Crooked Cops

The teacher shows the park announcement in class; cops arrest Dario too and shake Hugh down. In juvie, an overheard rumor cuts away to the Roosters pressuring the mayor over it — the first use of a device the film will lean on through Hugh’s whole missing decade.

The teacher shows the mayor’s park announcement on the news in class — built right around the wall — a good day, briefly. Then the cops walk in: Dario’s been arrested too, an accomplice to Junior’s charge, and they want money from Hugh to make it go away. He won’t pay. They arrest him as an accomplice himself — landing him in juvie alongside Junior and Dario both — not knowing Nina and Vera are close enough to catch the whole shakedown on tape. Inside, Hugh overhears two Roosters already locked up, killing time on the park news. One thinks it was a nice gesture from the mayor. The other snorts — that’s not how he heard it happened. Cut to the mayor’s office: the Roosters cornering him, wanting to know why he announced a new park without clearing it with the guys upstairs. He’s rattled, almost sheepish — he doesn’t fully know himself. It just came to him in a dream, felt right, and he woke up fired up about it. That’s as far as his explanation goes. The Roosters trade a look: an opportunity, not a threat — a park is just more playground for them too. Reyes, quiet until now, delivers the last word: good thing for the mayor that the guys upstairs found a way to profit off his little moment of inspiration, or he’d be finding out what happens to people who go off script.

♪ 8. Juvie Art

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — transformation number; Junior openly schemes to scare Hugh off, and it works at first, before the room turns and rips a drawing apart instead.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a transformation number that starts sparse and builds as each kid joins in, one by one, ending as a full group sound. Hugh draws for the other kids inside, a line forms. But Junior gets there first: furious Hugh gave up the bail money and still landed him here anyway, he works the room against him on purpose — needling, turning kids who don't know Hugh yet, making sure the first thing anyone in this place learns about him is not to trust him. It works. Hugh eats alone the first night, gets shoved once, nobody steps in. Then the drawing starts catching on anyway, kid by kid, despite Junior — feuding inmates mellow, a sad kid smiles, a shy and scared one laughs and jokes for the first time in weeks, the gift working even here, winning the room back one at a time. Junior, watching his scheme come apart and furious about it, rips up a drawing Hugh had given Dario in front of everyone — the ugliest he gets. Someone mutters it looked like it was about to jump right off the page — nobody thinks twice about it.

9. The Twins' Rescue

Nina and Vera show up with the recording; Hugh's cleared.

Nina and Vera come forward with the recording — the cops demanding a bribe, caught on tape — and Hugh is cleared.

10. Midpoint Montage

The park rises, a modest win. But the Roosters are moving in — using it as new turf for their rackets. Reyes works another angle: dread that sends shop owners running straight into their arms.

The park rises — modest, real, a small win. But beneath it, the Roosters and Raccoons are already moving in — using the new gathering place as fresh turf: running bolita numbers off the benches, collecting "insurance" from vendors setting up nearby, folding it straight into the same operation squeezing the rest of the block. Reyes works a different angle: the same dread from beat 3, planted now behind one shop owner's eyes after another — a formless, specific terror that something's coming for everything they've built. Owners wake in cold sweats, and the Roosters are there within days, offering exactly the safety the dream promised, never mentioning they're the ones who sent it. Hugh dodges the crew, tension mounting from every side. MIDPOINT.

♪ 11. The Racetrack

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — Hugh, Prince, Dario, and the twins sneak into the racetrack together and overhear the Buyers' villain song, aimed at the mayor. Reyes is there too, unexplained.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the villain song, but no longer performed to an empty room: the whole kid crew, Hugh, Prince, Dario, and the twins, sneak into the racetrack together on a dare and work their way up toward the private boxes, and that's where they catch it. The Buyers are working the mayor, a tense negotiation over zoning and permits that tips into song, a slinky, catchy, quietly horrifying number laying out who they really are and how they run the block from a box nobody downstairs ever sees. In the room with them, saying little: Reyes, unexplained, unsettling, something dark clinging to him the kids don't understand yet. They get out before anyone catches them, but now five kids are carrying a secret too big for them, and each will handle the weight of it differently.

12. The Ultimatum

Dario confronts Hugh: they know about the break-in, join the Raccoons or else. Dario joins. Hugh refuses and runs.

Dario corners Hugh, rattled — the Roosters know they broke into the racetrack, and the only way out is in: join the Raccoons, or else. Dario gives in, the pressure from above real and immediate. Hugh refuses and runs instead, the weight of what they saw now sitting on him alone.

♪ 13. The Fire

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — wistful elegy, breaks apart as smoke invades the vision and real fire takes over; Hugh collapses into his mom's arms, grandmother's cryptic warning. Rebuilt at The Realization.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a wistful elegy, possibly reprising Hugh's earlier melody now played tender and alone; the song itself breaks apart as smoke creeps into the vision — not rebuilt until "The Realization" (16). Hugh alone in the studio, pulled once more into that same magical painted escape, warm and alive — until smoke starts drifting through it, wrong, uninvited. He surfaces to find himself surrounded by real fire and bursts free, sweaty and frantic, collapsing into his mother's arms outside. She's panicking, shaking him, asking why he stayed in there, why he didn't come out, she thought she'd lost him. Prince's grandmother comes rushing over too, having seen the smoke. Hugh says he was stuck in his stupid fantasy world thing. She tells him it's not stupid. Mom, confused, asks what they're talking about, and Hugh deflects — it doesn't matter, he's never doing that again. The grandmother pushes back: don't say that, it'll be okay, he just needs to learn to control it somewhere safe, don't forget who he is — then storms off, leaving the words hanging. They flee together to the airport. PLOT POINT TWO: flight to Phoenix. Prince sees him go, watching from a distance, saying nothing, just a sad, quiet look as his friend disappears. On the plane, Hugh watches the city fall away and says nothing — he doesn't know about the bird, or the park idea it planted, only that whatever that door in the shed was, he's decided to nail it shut. Hialeah shrinks into cloud, and something in him goes quiet the same way. Music DNA: 70% Celia Cruz & Willie Colón (horns like electric guitars, aggressive trombone) for raw energy breaking apart, with a modern edge of El Taiger's reparto fury underneath as it turns dark. Listening: "Apaga La Luz" (Celia & Willie), El Taiger.

ACT TWO B — THE EMPTY CLIMB

14. The Long Climb

One unbroken montage, strictly Hugh's point of view, across the whole ten years in Arizona — the hustle, meeting Judy, the restaurant's rise, growing more crushing as it climbs.

TEN YEARS, told as one continuous montage, and we never leave Hugh's side to check in on Hialeah — the audience is as cut off from home as he is. The hustle building from nothing into something real: the restaurant taking off, meeting Judy along the way. It plays like a win at first, then starts tightening, the wins costing more than they used to, the days getting fuller and the nights emptier, success curdling slowly into something crushing. The rise never stops looking like progress, even as it starts to look like avoidance. It climbs, and climbs, and just as it peaks, it breaks — into the news that his mother has died, and the realization, mid-stride, that he's about to miss saying goodbye to her.

15. The Funeral He Missed

The montage breaks into real time. Mom's funeral, missed; sorting her things, the old photo, his father's letters; a real conversation with Judy pushes him toward reckoning with everything he's been outrunning.

The montage slows to real time right as it breaks him. Hugh misses the funeral, buries the guilt in what's left of the work that got him here. Sorting his mother's things afterward, he finds an old photo of himself as a boy, paint-stained, mid-brushstroke, and letters from his father, still in Cuba, that he's never seen. A real conversation with Judy cuts through the noise he's been living in. Alone in the desert, he finally has to sit with everything he's been outrunning.

♪ 16. The Realization

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — positive flip of The Fire's broken melody, rebuilding note by note as the decision to go home takes shape.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the positive flip of "The Fire"'s broken melody: the same tune, now rebuilding note by note as a mirror-doodle resolves into his decision. Still in the desert, still turning everything from the funeral over in his head, he catches himself doodling without noticing — in the dust on a shelf, on a napkin, in the fog on the bathroom mirror. Her words from years ago drift back, tangled with his father's letters and a flash of himself as a kid with a paintbrush. He wipes the fog away and actually sees himself for the first time in ten years. Judy's the one who finally says it: go home. Cut to him telling her they're going back to Hialeah. Music DNA: 50% Silvio Rodríguez (acoustic, intimate, guitar rebuilding fragments) as the melody reassembles note by note, 30% Beny Moré bolero elegance turning to healing. Listening: Silvio Rodríguez, Beny Moré.

ACT THREE — THE RETURN

17. Homecoming

The block overrun, racetrack untouched; the truth that the mayor's been in on it for years surfaces at the reunion, Ray supplies gear, Junior/Dario grown, a real blowup with Prince — and the whole missing decade starts coming out through catching up.

Driving in, the racetrack still stands pristine behind its walls, untouched. The park overrun — the wall's paint long faded. Domino games and youth sports by day give way to neutral ground for rival crews after dark. The old domino players still watching, still saying nothing. A wrongness under the surface. Warm but salty toward Judy at the reunion — reveal that the mayor isn't just quiet, he's in on it, his zoning favors and development approvals the reason the racket's never once been touched, the kind of thing that travels fastest at the ventana. Ray quietly stocks the community studio with professional paint and gear, happy to supply what he can — his answer to years of staying quiet, said without a word. As he talks, a glimpse he doesn't shy from: Junior and Dario, grown now, part of the Roosters' crew. Prince is there too — and it boils over fast. Years of silence, no calls, no letters, nothing, while Prince carried the fight alone. Hugh has no good answer. It's ugly before it's anything else, and Prince walks off before Hugh can even try to fix it. Everything Hugh missed — the twins' long investigation, Willy's situation, Prince's years of sabotage, Ray's careful distance — starts surfacing from here on, piece by piece, through catching up rather than anything Hugh, or we, watched happen in real time.

♪ 18. The Gift Reignites

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — communal joy, working through the cold aftermath of the argument with Prince.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the movie sings again for the first time since "Left Behind"; a big communal joy number, probably the film's most purely fun sequence, though it doesn't start easy. Hugh grieves at the old apartment, gets the art supplies — and finds Prince there too, still cold from the blowup, working the family's figures alongside his grandmother nearby, sticking around anyway because the wall needs it more than either of them needs to be right. Old rhythm returning despite everything, and by the number's end, Prince names what Hugh's gift really is: not rare, just what happens whenever anyone brightens the block, no matter how they choose to fight for it. Not forgiveness yet. But a start. The painted world returns, bigger than ever — his mom present within it. He discovers Willy's charter company is now a laundering front for the Buyers' money — and he and Ray launch a public art club: no walls of their own, supplies staged out of Ray's shop, the work spilling straight into the park itself — murals, replanting, relandscaping. Celebrated with a backyard lechón and salsa night, joy as defiance before the pressure escalates.

19. The Erasure

Color keeps going up around the block; the Raccoons keep quietly whitewashing it away.

It starts small and keeps happening: whatever color goes up around the block — a mural, a stretch of chalk art, a painted fence — is whitewashed within a day or two, quietly, no one ever seen doing it. Not the block rising up together yet, just erosion, one wall at a time, the Raccoons clearing the way for the Buyers block by block. It plants the dread that's about to turn explicit.

20. The Same Craft

Reyes recognizes the sabotage Prince has been pulling off for years and confronts him directly, alone — not as a threat but a warning. The truth comes out: same family craft, same grandmother, a mother who refused the Buyers and Roosters and paid for it — and for the first time, a real, fragile reconciliation between them.

Reyes finds Prince alone. He's not here as muscle, not exactly — he recognized the sabotage the moment the Roosters described the melted figure lodged in that engine, because he knows exactly what it takes to shape one with a purpose like that. He tells Prince, flatly, to knock it off before the Roosters do worse: quit interfering with the craft, or the whole family goes on a boat to nowhere, same as families made to vanish for a generation. Prince, stunned that his uncle even knows the craft, pushes back — and it comes out all at once, not as a confession Reyes wanted to give but one wrung out of him: the family craft isn't just Prince's grandmother's, it's Reyes's too, passed down the same line — the same figures, meant to protect and heal, that Reyes has spent years forcing to carry harm instead. His sister — Prince's mother — refused to turn it against her own community when the Buyers and Roosters first came calling, and she and her husband were put on a boat to nowhere for it. Reyes, faced with the same threat, gave in instead, and has spent every year since protecting the one family member he had left — his mother, Prince's grandmother — under that same standing threat. Do this or she disappears. Prince has spent years not knowing why his uncle became what he became, or that the two of them share a gift at all. Now he does — and hearing himself say it out loud, watching Prince's face change from suspicion to something closer to recognition, breaks something loose in Reyes he'd kept locked down for years. He says the thing he's never let himself say: he's sorry, and he's proud of what Prince has done, even if he was never able to help. It's not forgiveness yet — years of silence and menace don't undo that fast — but it's real, both of them meaning it. Behind them, the mural on the wall Prince painted stirs, just slightly, a small figure lifting its head and settling again, gone before either of them thinks to look. The threat over his mother hasn't gone anywhere, and Reyes knows it — whatever this was between them, it doesn't make him free.

21. The Standoff

Epic battle: painted creatures, old and new as residents catch on and join in, rise up against bulldozers and earth movers — Reyes forced to drive the machines himself, unable to finish the last one. Hugh goes down.

Reyes chants, arms raised, and the Roosters' bulldozers and earth movers grind to life under his command — he's not fighting anyone, just doing what he's been ordered to do, tearing into the restored park to clear ground for the racetrack's expansion, new parking and a bigger private box the Buyers have been planning for months. Ray's shop caught in the wreckage at the park's edge. Every mural, every painted thing the block has ever made rises to fight back — years of communal work turned into an army — and more keep joining: residents catching on to what's happening grab whatever they've got, chalk, paint, sugar and wax, throwing new creatures into the fight as fast as they can make them, the roster swelling by the minute. Painted and molded and chalked things of every shape and size against raw machinery. Epic, outcome uncertain, more fun than frightening even at its most dangerous. Dario sides with Hugh and is shoved off a roof for it. Willy hands his evidence straight to Nina and Vera, mid-chaos, badge and briefcase both right there in the wreckage — the missing piece that finally gets them back in the fight after backing off in beat 19. Judy right in the middle of it, all in. Mid-chaos, his mom's painted image comes alive — her lips don't move, but he hears her anyway: "Mommy's gotta work, baby. Be good while I'm gone." Reyes, driving the last machine toward the one house still standing — his own mother's house — finally can't do it. His hands freeze on the controls, the machine stalling out beneath him. The creatures swarm past him and finish what he couldn't bring himself to start. He slips away in the chaos, nobody sees where. Then Hugh goes down.

♪ 22. Adrift

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — the guiding music IS the song. At rock bottom, he paints his mom in spilled water and confesses everything.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the plot mechanism and the number are the same event: the faint music he follows home grows from a distant fragment into a full song as land comes into view. Hugh wakes on a boat already speeding out to sea, no memory of how he got there. For a moment he wonders if he's dead too. No art power at first. He burns precious fuel hunting for land, dehydrated, sends up his one flare in the night at a distant boat — wasted, unseen. Fuel gone. Another boat passes at night; he chases it till the engine dies, jumps in, swims for its wake. Alone, floating in the dark sea. At his lowest, he paints a tiny figure in spilled water on the deck — his mom, the only material he has left. She comes to life, small enough to do nothing but hug his arm, and listens while he confesses everything: how mad he was at her, how much he missed her, how he swore he'd never work himself into the ground the way she did — and is doing it anyway, hates himself for it. Then — faint music. He swims toward it, loses it, finds it again, homes in as it grows louder. City lights. He barely makes it. The music is the park, alive again with the community's repainting — the same current that mellowed the boys in juvie, now pulling him home. Music DNA: every melody converges here — Los Van Van's current, Beny Moré's rebuilt line, Tres Coronas' bright son cubano, Celia & Willie's salsa defiance, Silvio Rodríguez's intimate acoustic, and Buena Vista Social Club's nostalgic warmth, all pulling him home at once. Listening: "Cómo Fue" (Beny Moré), Buena Vista Social Club.

23. Resolution

Courtroom takedown of the mayor, the Buyers, and the Roosters together, built on evidence from the whole block — Willy, Ray, and the Juans all take the stand; Reyes vanishes; Dario recovers, watching — the park saved for good.

Back on shore, Hugh catches the news: Nina and Vera, done waiting on a mayor who was never going to move on his own, took everything straight to the state attorney instead — and it's brought the whole racket down at once. Willy hands over the books tying the bolita and protection money to the mayor's own zoning approvals. Ray takes the stand and admits what he paid and why, quick and unflinching. The Juans show up too, back on camera for the first time since being forced out, telling the world exactly what it cost them. The mayor goes down right alongside the Buyers and the Roosters, no separate ending for him, no redemption, just named on the same indictment. Reyes' name comes up too, wanted for questioning, but he's nowhere to be found. Dario, recovering in a hospital bed, watches the same coverage. The racetrack's expansion plans are dead on arrival — the land stays the park's, for good. Cut to the block gathered there instead, the mural at its heart still standing after the fight, the cheer going up equal parts relief and disbelief that it actually worked. A quiet flash, tucked into the celebration: Prince gets word, or maybe just a feeling, that his grandmother is safe somewhere far from all of it — Reyes got her out before he vanished, the one thing he could still do right. Nobody chases it further. The full community restored — the park handed back to the block for good, the wall's mural at its heart: his mom, and young Hugh mid-brushstroke, straight from the photo. At the racetrack, the Buyers' private box sits empty — the staff who used to serve it finally watching the race themselves, no one left to answer to. The painted world shared by the whole town, the old neighbor and Prince among them — Judy home in it too.

24. Button

Willy’s boats running clean and full of friends — the mural, with both boys’ tools nowhere in it — and Hugh telling Judy they’re going back to Arizona, not to run this time, but to finish the fight there too.

Out on the water, Willy's boats running clean and busy again — Hugh, Prince, Judy, the twins, the whole crew of them out there for no reason but the pleasure of it, loud and laughing, the charter-boat dream from beat 1 finally just a normal afternoon. Then the last image: the wall. The mural finished, Hugh's mom and young Hugh mid-brushstroke at its heart — and no paintbrush in Hugh's hand anywhere near it, no figures in Prince's hands. The tools are down. Neither of them is holding the current anymore, because the whole block is. Hugh turns to Judy, the wall behind them, and says the last line of the film: "Now we go back to Arizona." Not running this time — going back to do there what they just did here.
