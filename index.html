ACT ONE — THE GIFT

♪ 1. A Morning in Hialeah

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — opening ensemble: Hialeah's world, Hugh's hustle, and its power structure.

MUSICAL NUMBER — one big opening ensemble number weaving together Hialeah's world, Hugh's hustle, and its power structure. 12-year-old Hugh. Establishes Hialeah's warmth and hard edges — new apartment complexes creeping in block by block, swallowing houses whole. Walls everywhere — garage doors, fences, the sides of small shops — all get painted over and repainted, a rotating canvas across the neighborhood. The park becomes a gathering place for the same impulse, chalk art on concrete, murals on every surface the block can claim. Mom's "I'm proof your hard work matters — but remember to slow down, enjoy this life too" line carries the urgency of her own Mariel-generation arrival. Hugh's promise to do great things. His jobs plant Willy at the marina and the shared charter-boat dream, and how thin the line is between the hustle and outright theft. But his art — the drawing, the painting, the shed — stays private, an escape he doesn't share. A quick glimpse of Nina and Vera in the crowd, twin girls who miss nothing — trading a look every time something on the block doesn't add up, already the neighborhood's unofficial eyes and ears. Roosters and Raccoons established as the neighborhood's street-level power.

2. Ray's Shop

Ray's early warmth toward Hugh — and something a little off, unexplained yet.

Ray Cash's warmth toward Hugh planted early — personal, not just principled. But something's a little off: a tense exchange, a glance out the window, gone before Hugh really registers it.

♪ 3. The Buyers

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — the villain song, sung from the private box.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a slinky villain number for the Buyers, sung from their private box at the racetrack, catchy and quietly horrifying. A small group of wealthy men — with a hand on the zoning board — is quietly buying up Hialeah's cheap housing, permits rubber-stamped and violations ignored. Deals cut from a private box at the racetrack, untouched by anything happening below. Families keep selling fast, after nights of the same terrible dream nobody can explain — the block overrun by Roosters and Raccoons, dreamed before it's real. The real power above the Roosters and Raccoons, driving longtime residents out and pulling new ones in.

4. 3 Juan's Pizza

Hugh gets a free slice; fatherly warmth from the counter — and overhears the nightmare talk.

Hugh gets a free slice on his way home. Fatherly advice from behind the counter, no charge — another small, quiet source of warmth for a kid without one at home. Regulars trade worried talk over the register: another family on the block had the same bad dream, packed up and gone by morning.

5. Inciting Incident

Junior's arrested; Dario begs Hugh for bail money, and gets it — his AC dream, let go for a friend.

Junior arrested — the easy money finally catches up with him. Dario begs Hugh for bail money through the window. Hugh gives him what he's saved for the window AC unit anyway. His dream, let go for a friend.

♪ 6. The Painted World

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — Hugh's I Want song, a reaction to the day. Prince's grandmother reveals both boys' gifts at once, and names the difference between them.

MUSICAL NUMBER — Hugh's "I Want" song: wonder and longing as the gift shows itself for the first time, a melody "The Fire" will later reprise and break, and "The Gift Reignites" will finally answer. Still shaken from the day — Junior, the money, the ache of giving it up — Hugh retreats to his art shed, and that's when it happens: the shed transports him into the idealized Hialeah. Prince — already a friend, already living with his grandmother, parents gone, never explained — is there too. She's the one who recognizes it: a santera who's kept her family's craft alive for generations. She names Hugh's gift for what it is, and in the same breath finally lets Prince in on his own — small sugar figures, hand-molded, sealed with intent, the family's tradition passed down at last. What Prince has, she tells him, was taught, carried down, earned through lineage. What Hugh has wasn't taught to anyone — it's just love doing what the current always does when it's real. Something old and sad crosses her face when she says it doesn't always stay sweet. Neither boy understands what she means. Neither will, for years.

7. First Brush with Magic

A painted bird plants the park idea in the mayor's sleep.

A bird Hugh painted lifts off the canvas and flies to the mayor's window that night, whispering the park idea into his sleep. PLOT POINT ONE: the gift's first real ripple into the world.

ACT TWO A — CONSEQUENCES

8. Crooked Cops

The Roosters lean on the mayor over the park he can't explain; the teacher shows the announcement in class, then cops arrest Dario too and shake Hugh down.

The Roosters corner the mayor in his office, wanting to know why he announced a new park without clearing it with the guys upstairs. He's rattled, almost sheepish — he doesn't fully know himself. It just came to him in a dream, felt right, and he woke up fired up about it. That's as far as his explanation goes. The Roosters trade a look: an opportunity, not a threat — a park is just more playground for them too. Reyes, quiet until now, delivers the last word: good thing for the mayor that the guys upstairs found a way to profit off his little moment of inspiration, or he'd be finding out what happens to people who go off script. Cut to class, where the teacher shows the same park announcement on the news — built right around the wall — a good day, briefly. Then the cops walk in: Dario's been arrested too, an accomplice to Junior's charge, and they want money from Hugh to make it go away. He won't pay. They arrest him as an accomplice himself — landing him in juvie alongside Junior and Dario both — not knowing Nina and Vera are close enough to catch the whole shakedown on tape.

♪ 9. Juvie Art

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — transformation number; Junior openly schemes to scare Hugh off, and it works at first, before the room turns and rips a drawing apart instead.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a transformation number that starts sparse and builds as each kid joins in, one by one, ending as a full group sound. Hugh draws for the other kids inside, a line forms. But Junior gets there first: furious Hugh gave up the bail money and still landed him here anyway, he works the room against him on purpose — needling, turning kids who don't know Hugh yet, making sure the first thing anyone in this place learns about him is not to trust him. It works. Hugh eats alone the first night, gets shoved once, nobody steps in. Then the drawing starts catching on anyway, kid by kid, despite Junior — feuding inmates mellow, a sad kid smiles, a shy and scared one laughs and jokes for the first time in weeks, the gift working even here, winning the room back one at a time. Junior, watching his scheme come apart and furious about it, rips up a drawing Hugh had given Dario in front of everyone — the ugliest he gets. Someone mutters it looked like it was about to jump right off the page — nobody thinks twice about it.

10. The Twins' Rescue

Nina and Vera show up with the recording; Hugh's cleared.

Nina and Vera come forward with the recording — the cops demanding a bribe, caught on tape — and Hugh is cleared.

11. Midpoint Montage

The park rises; walls painted, undone within days, repainted. But the Roosters are moving in — using it as cover while they rob homes. Reyes works another angle: nightmares.

The park rises — modest, real, a small win. Kids paint the bare apartment walls, color into concrete. Within a few days, someone's painted over it. They do it again anyway. The cycle begins here. But beneath it, the Roosters and Raccoons are already moving in — using the new gathering place as cover for something else: robbing residents' homes, driving families out with theft and fear. Reyes works a different angle: nightmares planted in windows, residents waking in cold sweat, too shaken to stay. Hugh dodges the crew, tension mounting from every side. MIDPOINT.

12. The Demand

Dario begs Hugh to join the crew for protection. Hugh refuses and runs.

Dario begs Hugh to join the Roosters — the crew is pressing him hard, the pressure from above is real, and he needs Hugh to help him survive it.

♪ 13. The Fire

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — wistful elegy, breaks apart as the fire takes it. Rebuilt at The Realization.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a wistful elegy, possibly reprising Hugh's earlier melody now played tender and alone; the song itself breaks apart as the fire takes over — not rebuilt until "The Realization" (20). Hugh alone in the studio, the gift alive and warm one more time. Then it burns. PLOT POINT TWO: flight to Phoenix. On the plane, Mom, watching the city fall away: "A gift like yours isn't for keeping to yourself, mijo." Hugh shuts the door on Hialeah and painting anyway.

ACT TWO B — THE EMPTY CLIMB

14. Reinvention Montage

Ten years later: a safe, modest life in AZ with Judy — something's still missing.

TEN YEARS LATER. Hugh runs a successful landscaping business in Arizona — safe, steady, growing. He's good with his hands, good with people on the job, keeps the days structured and the nights empty. Judy's steady beside him, the safe, managed version of connection he allows himself. Any brush with art or color still stings, gets flinched away from; he hasn't touched a brush since the fire. Mom's house kept ice-cold, AC cranked no matter the bill — he never lets her be cold again, though she jokes she's always cold in the desert. Something's missing, and he can't say what.

♪ 15. Prince Carries On

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — sad reprise of the opening number, without Hugh. Prince sabotages the Buyers with sugar figures; Reyes grows suspicious.

MUSICAL NUMBER — a minor-key reprise of the opening number's melody ("Left Behind"), sung about Hialeah but without Hugh in it — the last song until "The Gift Reignites"; the film goes unscored through his whole Arizona arc as a deliberate structural choice: song equals connection to the current, and its absence is his exile made literal. Hialeah cutaway — Prince carries the fight alone, using magic of his own — small sugar figures, hand-molded and sealed with intent, not painting — to thwart the Buyers, the Roosters, and the Raccoons piece by piece over the years, sabotaging their equipment from the inside. The Roosters report the strange find back to Reyes: sugar, melted into the guts of a seized engine, no explanation for how it got there or why it stopped the machine cold. Small thing, but it's enough — Reyes starts to suspect him, though he doesn't yet know why it matters. The wall dims, touched up now and then by hands nobody names.

16. Losing Ground

Willy's business slips to the Roosters piece by piece; the mayor stays quiet through all of it.

Willy's charter business slips under the Roosters' grip, piece by piece — paperwork he doesn't remember signing, routes he doesn't control anymore. The mayor stays quiet through all of it, complicit in everything but name.

17. The Funeral He Missed

Mom passes; sorting her things, Hugh finds his father's letters from a Cuban prison.

Mom passes. Hugh buries the guilt in more work instead of feeling it, and misses the funeral too. Sorting her things after, he finds an old photo of himself as a boy, paint-stained, mid-brushstroke — one he can't look at long enough to keep or throw away. Underneath it, a bundle of letters he's never seen: his father's handwriting, postmarked from a prison in Cuba, addressed to his mother across years Hugh knew nothing about. Each one carries the same ache — missing them, missing Hialeah, a plan to get back that never quite arrives. The last letter is shorter than the rest: it's getting harder to have these smuggled out, he's not sure how many more will make it through, but he hasn't stopped trying to find his way home to his family. No date after it. No way to know what happened next. Hugh sits with a father he's never going to get to ask.

18. Ray's Balancing Act

Ray secretly pays off both sides to survive — juxtaposed against 3 Juan's, forced out entirely.

Back home, Ray stays warm to every local who walks in — and quietly pays off both the Roosters and the Buyers to keep his doors open. The register hints at money nobody in the neighborhood wants to ask about. Not everyone plays that game and survives it: 3 Juan's, the pizza counter that once gave a kid a free slice and fatherly advice, is boarded up and gone, the Juans forced out same as anyone who wouldn't or couldn't pay. Ray's still standing because he learned to bend. They didn't.

19. Scared Off

The twins keep building the case, until the nightmares find them too.

Nina and Vera keep building the case against the Buyers pro bono — until the nightmares start reaching them too. Each time, they back off a little further, no idea why.

♪ 20. The Realization

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — positive flip of The Fire's broken melody, rebuilding note by note.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the positive flip of "The Fire"'s broken melody: the same tune, now rebuilding note by note as the mirror-doodle resolves into his decision — mirroring how "Left Behind" flips "A Morning in Hialeah," the film's second mirrored song pair. Her words from the plane resurface — a gift like yours isn't for keeping to yourself — tangled up with Reyes's name and a flash of himself as a kid with a paintbrush. That night — or the next morning — he doodles in the fog on the bathroom mirror without thinking, wipes it away, and sees himself. Judy's the one who finally says it: go home. Cut to him telling her they're going back to Hialeah.

ACT THREE — THE RETURN

21. Homecoming

The block overrun, racetrack untouched; corruption revealed at the reunion, Ray supplies gear, Junior/Dario grown — and a real blowup with Prince.

Driving in, the racetrack still stands pristine behind its walls, untouched. The park overrun — the wall's paint long faded. Domino games and youth sports by day give way to neutral ground for rival crews after dark. The old domino players still watching, still saying nothing. A wrongness under the surface. Warm but salty toward Judy at the reunion — reveal of the mayor's corruption, the kind of thing that travels fastest at the ventana. Ray quietly stocks the community studio with professional paint and gear, happy to supply what he can — his answer to years of staying quiet, said without a word. As he talks, a glimpse he doesn't shy from: Junior and Dario, grown now, part of the Roosters' crew. Prince is there too — and it boils over fast. Years of silence, no calls, no letters, nothing, while Prince carried the fight alone. Hugh has no good answer. It's ugly before it's anything else, and Prince walks off before Hugh can even try to fix it.

♪ 22. The Gift Reignites

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — communal joy, working through the cold aftermath of the argument with Prince.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the movie sings again for the first time since "Left Behind"; a big communal joy number, probably the film's most purely fun sequence, though it doesn't start easy. Hugh grieves at the old apartment, gets the art supplies — and finds Prince there too, still cold from the blowup, working sugar figures alongside his grandmother nearby, sticking around anyway because the wall needs it more than either of them needs to be right. Old rhythm returning despite everything, and by the number's end, Prince names what Hugh's gift really is: not rare, just what happens whenever anyone brightens the block, no matter how they choose to fight for it. Not forgiveness yet. But a start. The painted world returns, bigger than ever — his mom present within it. He discovers Willy's charter company is now a laundering front for the Buyers' money — and he and Ray launch a public art club: no walls of their own, supplies staged out of Ray's shop, the work spilling straight into the park itself — murals, replanting, relandscaping. Celebrated with a backyard lechón and salsa night, joy as defiance before the pressure escalates.

23. The Erasure

The wall whitewashed overnight; the block repaints it together.

The Buyers make it personal — murals and chalk art across the neighborhood whitewashed overnight, erasing color block by block to push sales. By sunset the whole block turns out to repaint: not just the park, but every fence, garage, corner, and wall gets color back. The cycle becomes open, collective defiance.

24. Blood and Sugar

Reyes recognizes the sugar-figure sabotage from beat 15 and confronts Prince directly, alone — not as a threat but a warning: knock it off, or the Roosters put you all on a boat to nowhere. The truth comes out: same family gift, same grandmother, and a mother who refused the Buyers and Roosters and paid for it. Reyes still walks away Roosters' man.

Reyes finds Prince alone. He's not here as muscle, not exactly — he recognized the sabotage the moment the Roosters described the melted sugar in that engine, because he knows exactly what it takes to make sugar hold a shape and a purpose like that. He tells Prince, flatly, to knock it off before the Roosters do worse: quit interfering with the magic, or the whole family goes on a boat to nowhere, same as families made to vanish for a generation. Prince, stunned that his uncle even knows the craft, pushes back — and it comes out all at once, not as a confession Reyes wanted to give but one wrung out of him: the sugar-figure tradition isn't just Prince's grandmother's, it's Reyes's too, passed down the same line. His sister — Prince's mother — refused to turn it against her own community when the Buyers and Roosters first came calling, and she and her husband were put on a boat to nowhere for it. Reyes, faced with the same threat, gave in instead, and has spent every year since protecting the one family member he had left — Prince's grandmother — under that same standing threat. Do this or she disappears. Prince has spent years not knowing why his uncle became what he became, or that the two of them share a gift at all. Now he does — and it doesn't fix anything. Reyes isn't here to reconcile; he's here because he's still trapped, still the Roosters' man, and the warning stands. He leaves Prince with the threat hanging and walks back to the people he answers to.

25. The Standoff

Epic battle: painted creatures vs. animated machinery. Reyes sees his dead sister alive in paint, breaks, and slips away — Roosters' silhouettes close in behind him. Hugh goes down.

Reyes chants, arms raised, and the Roosters' bulldozers and earth movers grind to life on their own — simple, single-minded, tearing into the restored park and the holdout houses. Ray's shop caught in the wreckage. Every mural, every painted thing the block has ever made rises to fight back: years of communal work turned into an army, imagined creatures against raw machinery. Epic, outcome uncertain. Dario sides with Hugh and is shoved off a roof for it. Prince versus Reyes — nephew against uncle, sugar figures against splintered stick dolls, sweet against crooked, a debt years overdue. Willy hands his evidence straight to Nina and Vera, mid-chaos — the missing piece that finally gets them back in the fight after backing off in beat 19. Judy right in the middle of it, all in. Mid-chaos, his mom's painted image comes alive — her lips don't move, but he hears her anyway: "Mommy's gotta work, baby. Be good while I'm gone." Reyes sees her too, mid-chant, and freezes — his sister, alive in paint, the same as the day she was taken. He watches her run off and barrel straight into a bulldozer, taking it down before she evaporates like every other creature on the wall. It's enough. Reyes stops chanting, stops fighting, and slips away in the chaos — nobody sees where. With him gone, nobody's protecting Hugh anymore: Roosters' silhouettes close in behind him out of the smoke and floodlight, shadows falling long across the wreckage before he even turns around. Then Hugh goes down.

♪ 26. Adrift

♪ MUSICAL NUMBER — the guiding music IS the song. At rock bottom, he paints his mom in spilled water and confesses everything.

MUSICAL NUMBER — the plot mechanism and the number are the same event: the faint music he follows home grows from a distant fragment into a full song as land comes into view. Hugh wakes on a boat already speeding out to sea, no memory of how he got there. For a moment he wonders if he's dead too. No art power at first. He burns precious fuel hunting for land, dehydrated, sends up his one flare in the night at a distant boat — wasted, unseen. Fuel gone. Another boat passes at night; he chases it till the engine dies, jumps in, swims for its wake. Alone, floating in the dark sea. At his lowest, he paints a tiny figure in spilled water on the deck — his mom, the only material he has left. She comes to life, small enough to do nothing but hug his arm, and listens while he confesses everything: how mad he was at her, how much he missed her, how he swore he'd never work himself into the ground the way she did — and is doing it anyway, hates himself for it. Then — faint music. He swims toward it, loses it, finds it again, homes in as it grows louder. City lights. He barely makes it. The music is the park, alive again with the community's repainting — the same current that mellowed the boys in juvie, now pulling him home.

27. Resolution

Courtroom takedown; Ray confesses, Reyes vanishes; Dario recovers, watching — one of the Buyers' own shoddy towers condemned and demolished, the block cheering as the land goes back to single family homes for the displaced.

Back on shore, Hugh catches the news: the twins' courtroom takedown of the mayor, the Roosters, and the Buyers together — Ray among those on the stand, admitting his own part in it, quick and unflinching. Reyes' name comes up too, wanted for questioning, but he's nowhere to be found. Dario, recovering in a hospital bed, watches the same coverage. Buried in the ruling, a detail that lands hardest of all: inspectors finally condemn one of the Buyers' own rushed-up towers, the shoddy construction from beat one catching up with them at last — unsafe to inhabit, ordered down. Cut to the block gathered at a safe distance as the building comes down in a cloud of dust, and the cheer that goes up isn't just relief — they're told the land goes back to single family homes, first offer going to the families displaced to make room for it in the first place. A quiet flash, tucked into the celebration: Prince gets word, or maybe just a feeling, that his grandmother is safe somewhere far from all of it — Reyes got her out before he vanished, the one thing he could still do right. Nobody chases it further. The full community restored — the park handed back to the block, the wall's mural at its heart: his mom, and young Hugh mid-brushstroke, straight from the photo. At the racetrack, the Buyers' private box sits empty — the staff who used to serve it finally watching the race themselves, no one left to answer to. The painted world shared by the whole town, the old neighbor and Prince among them — Judy home in it too.

28. Button

Willy’s boats running clean and full of friends — then the mural, with both boys’ tools nowhere in it.

Out on the water, Willy's boats running clean and busy again — Hugh, Prince, Judy, the twins, the whole crew of them out there for no reason but the pleasure of it, loud and laughing, the charter-boat dream from beat 1 finally just a normal afternoon. Then the last image: the wall. The mural finished, Hugh's mom and young Hugh mid-brushstroke at its heart — and no paintbrush in Hugh's hand anywhere near it, no sugar figures in Prince's. The tools are down. Neither of them is holding the current anymore, because the whole block is.
