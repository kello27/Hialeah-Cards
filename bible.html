Premise

A boy in Hialeah discovers his paintings can come alive — a gift born purely of love, not lineage — just as his neighborhood starts disappearing block by block to wealthy buyers, corrupt cops, and street enforcers who profit from displacement. He runs from it for ten years. He comes home to fight for it, and to find out what the gift actually costs the people who carry it.

World

Hialeah, Miami — a working-class Cuban-American neighborhood built by the Mariel generation, rendered with real warmth and real hard edges. New apartment complexes creep in block by block, swallowing houses whole. Every wall, garage door, fence, and shop front doubles as a rotating public canvas — chalk art, murals, paint repainted almost as fast as it's covered over. That cycle of erasure and repainting is the neighborhood's heartbeat and the film's central visual metaphor: a community that keeps insisting on color no matter how many times it gets whitewashed.

Above the neighborhood sits the racetrack, its private boxes untouched by anything happening on the ground — the visual shorthand for money that never has to look at what it costs.

Era: the story opens in 1980. The "ten years later" jump lands Act Two B around 1990, and Act Three follows shortly after — no cell phones, no video calls; evidence travels on cassette tape, news travels by word of mouth and the local broadcast, and distance is felt for real (a flight to Phoenix, a letter smuggled out of Cuba, a courtroom win that takes years to build).

Themes

Community as a living, collective art project — the wall belongs to everyone; the ending is defined by nobody holding the paintbrush anymore, because the whole block is.
Displacement & erasure — gentrification isn't background, it's the antagonist's actual business model, enacted through zoning, debt, fear, and literal whitewashing.
The gift as a double-edged inheritance — love-given power (Hugh) versus lineage-given power (Prince and Reyes, same grandmother) — and what each person chooses to do with it under pressure. The grandmother's warning that the gift "doesn't always stay sweet" pays off across all three.
Running vs. staying — Hugh flees to Arizona; Prince stays and fights alone; Hugh's father never made it back at all, last heard from in a Cuban prison. The film doesn't moralize about which choice is right so much as insist on the cost of the ones left holding the fight.
Coercion, not corruption — the story's two "villain-adjacent" men close to our heroes, Reyes and (in a smaller way) Ray, are revealed to be surviving under threat rather than acting from greed. The real greed sits higher up, at the racetrack box.
Art as defiance — painting over the whitewashing, repainting the wall by sunset, the public art club spilling into the park: joy and color used deliberately as resistance, not just decoration.

Magic System

Hugh's gift (painting): untaught, inherited from no one — "just love doing what the current always does when it's real." What he paints can come alive and act in the world (the bird that plants the park idea in the mayor's sleep; the wall's creatures that fight in the climax). It first appears at his emotional lowest point in Act One and goes dormant the moment he shuts painting out of his life after the fire.
Prince's gift (sugar figures): taught, carried down through his grandmother's family line as santera tradition — small figures hand-molded and sealed with intent. Used for sabotage and protection rather than spectacle; quieter and more targeted than Hugh's.
Reyes's gift, same lineage: the grandmother passed the sugar-figure craft down to both her children — Reyes and his sister, Prince's mother. The gift itself isn't dark; it's the same inheritance Prince carries. What differs is the choice made under threat: Prince's mother refused to turn her figures against her own community and was put on the boat to nowhere for it, along with her husband. Reyes, her brother, gave in instead, and has used the family craft against Hialeah ever since — cursed figures that plant nightmares, and at full force, the ability to chant machinery to life.
The current: the film's name for the shared source under all three gifts — explicitly the reason the film goes unscored during Hugh's Arizona exile (song = connection to the current; its absence is his exile made literal) and explicitly what "wakes" at moments of real emotional truth (the reunion painting session; his mother's spirit rising to fight during the Standoff).
Rule of origin: gifts come from love (Hugh) or lineage (Prince and Reyes, from the same grandmother). The real divide isn't where the power comes from — it's what a person is willing to do with it under pressure. Prince's mother refused and paid with her life; Reyes complied and has been paying in guilt ever since.

Factions

The Buyers

Money & Power

A small group of wealthy men with a hand on the zoning board, quietly buying up Hialeah's cheap housing. Deals are cut from a private box at the racetrack, untouched by anything happening below. They finance the displacement but never do their own dirty work — that's the Roosters' job. Their laundering runs through Willy's charter company by Act Three.

The Roosters

On-the-Ground Enforcers

Street-level muscle and the neighborhood's direct power. They shake down residents, run protection rackets, coerce Reyes into turning his family's own gift against his own community, and animate the machinery in the final battle. The ones who actually corner Hugh and Prince with the boat-to-nowhere threat.

The Raccoons

Home-Invasion Crew

Work alongside the Roosters but with a specific MO: breaking into homes to steal valuables and plant Reyes's cursed figures, which invoke the nightmares that drive families out fast, without anyone understanding why.

Reyes

Coerced Enforcer

Prince's uncle by blood — his mother's brother, and the santera grandmother's son. When the Buyers and Roosters came for the family gift, Prince's mother refused to turn it against her own community and was put on the boat to nowhere for it, along with her husband. Reyes gave in instead, and has used the family's sugar-figure craft against Hialeah ever since, all to keep the same threat off his mother's back. When the Roosters report melted sugar found in a sabotaged engine, Reyes recognizes the craft immediately — and realizes, before anyone else does, that Prince has the gift too. He confronts Prince directly, alone, not as a threat but as a warning: stop messing with the machines, or the family goes on a boat to nowhere. The truth comes out, but it doesn't reconcile anything — Reyes is still trapped, still the Roosters' man, and he leaves the warning standing. It's not until the Standoff that he finally breaks: chanting the Buyers' machinery to life, he sees his dead sister alive in paint, watches her take down a bulldozer and vanish, and can't keep fighting. He slips away mid-battle and disappears for good — his one redemptive act getting his mother somewhere safe before he goes.

The Mayor

Complicit Silence

Rubber-stamps permits, ignores violations, stays quiet through all of it — complicit in everything but name. His only real "action" in the story, the park, is one he doesn't even know he was nudged into by Hugh's painted bird.

Characters

Hugh

Protagonist

12 in Act One, early-20s by the return. A hustler with a soft private streak — his art stays hidden even from friends. Gives up his own dream (a window AC unit, later implicitly the charter-boat dream) for people he loves without a second thought, then spends the back half of the film learning that self-sacrifice isn't the same as connection. Runs from the gift after the fire; the whole Arizona stretch of the film is him trying to outrun what he is.

Prince

Best Friend & Co-Lead

Raised by his grandmother; his mother (Reyes's sister) and father were put on the boat to nowhere for refusing to use the family gift against their own community, the same night Reyes gave in instead. Carries the sugar-figure tradition and, unlike Hugh, never leaves — fights the Buyers, Roosters, and Raccoons alone for ten years while the film's score itself goes silent around him. His blowup at Hugh during the reunion is earned: years of silence versus years of solo, unglamorous resistance. By the end, he's the one who names what Hugh's gift really is — not rare, just what happens when anyone brightens the block.

Prince's Grandmother

Santera / Mentor

Keeps her family's sugar-figure craft alive across generations. The one who recognizes and names both boys' gifts in the same scene — Prince's taught and inherited, Hugh's untaught and love-born — and the only one who senses early that the gift "doesn't always stay sweet." The person Reyes has spent years secretly protecting.

Reyes

Coerced Enforcer / Prince's Uncle

See Factions. Emotionally, he's the film's tragedy-in-miniature: a man who became what everyone fears him as, purely to keep one person safe. His confession is wrung out of him, not offered — and it's what finally lets Prince let go of years of not understanding him.

Dario

Friend

The one whose bail money Hugh gives up in the inciting incident, setting the whole plot rolling. Gets pressured into the Roosters over the course of the Empty Climb years. Sides with Hugh the moment it counts in the climax and pays for it — shoved off a roof — but survives to watch the courtroom takedown from a hospital bed.

Junior

Friend Turned Rival

Arrested first in the inciting incident; his easy money finally catches up with him. Turns on Hugh in juvie out of real, specific fury (Hugh gave up the bail money and Junior still ended up locked up anyway) rather than simple villainy. Grows into the Roosters' crew alongside Dario.

Ray (Ray Cash)

Shop Owner

Warm to Hugh from the start, in a way that's personal rather than just principled. Survives the AZ years by quietly paying off both the Roosters and the Buyers — a compromise that keeps his doors open while it costs him something he never talks about. Restocks the community studio with gear once Hugh's back, glad to supply what he can, and later takes the stand in court to admit his own part in it, unflinching.

3 Juan's Pizza, the counter that once gave Hugh a free slice, doesn't survive the same game — boarded up and gone by Act Two B, the contrast to Ray's survival.

Willy

Marina / Charter Boats

Hugh plants the shared charter-boat dream with him back in Act One. His business slips out from under him piece by piece during the AZ years — paperwork he doesn't remember signing, routes he doesn't control — and by Act Three it's a laundering front for the Buyers' money. Hands over the evidence that helps take them down, and gets his clean, full boats back by the button.

Perpetually wired on coffee — sight-gag potential in his big panda paws dwarfing the tiny cups he drinks from.

Judy

Hugh's Partner (Arizona)

The safe, managed version of connection Hugh allows himself after the fire. Steady rather than a fully drawn arc of her own in early acts, but the pivotal voice at the Realization — she's the one who actually tells him to go home. Fully present and all-in once they return, including in the climactic battle.

The Twins — Nina & Vera

Lawyers

Glimpsed early in the opening number — twin girls who miss nothing, already trading looks whenever something on the block doesn't add up. Catch the cops' bribery shakedown on tape in Act Two A, clearing Hugh. Spend the Empty Climb years building a pro bono case against the Buyers, until the nightmares start reaching them too and they're scared into backing off. Get back in the fight during the Standoff when Willy hands them the evidence they were missing, and deliver the courtroom takedown that resolves the whole plot in Act Three.

Finish each other's sentences and meow in unison. Whenever one is speaking, the other is so in sync she silently pantomimes the message alongside — gestures and expressions shadowing whatever's being said.

Mom

Hugh's Mother

Mariel-generation arrival; her "I'm proof your hard work matters, but remember to slow down" line sets Hugh's whole throughline of self-sacrifice versus rest. Dies while Hugh's in Arizona; he misses the funeral. Returns as a painted, spoken presence in the Act Three climax and again, made of spilled water, at Hugh's lowest point adrift at sea.

Hugh's Father

Absent Parent

Never appears on screen. Hugh learns his story only in fragments, found after Mom's death: a bundle of letters, postmarked from a prison in Cuba, tracing years Hugh never knew about and a plan to get home that never arrives — the last letter shorter than the rest, no date after it, no way to know what happened next. Unconnected to Reyes, Prince, or the family gift; his fate is left genuinely unresolved. Hugh is left piecing together a father he'll never get to ask.

Tone & Visual Direction

Warmth and hard edges, always both at once. Hialeah is never just charming or just grim — free pizza slices and fatherly counter-advice sit right next to zoning corruption and nightmare-induced displacement, often in the same scene.
The wall as a visual metronome. Painted, faded, whitewashed, repainted — track its state across the film as a silent gauge of who currently has the upper hand.
Magical realism, grounded. The gift shows up as an extension of real emotional stakes (grief, love, defiance), never as spectacle for its own sake. The animated creatures in the climax are the community's own years of painted labor, not a new power appearing from nowhere.
Color as mood, not just palette. Hugh's Arizona years should read visually muted/desaturated to match the unscored silence of that stretch; Hialeah, especially post-return, should be as saturated and layered as the murals themselves.
Water as throughline. Boats, the marina, the racetrack's proximity to the coast, and the Adrift sequence all tie back to a cold open POV shot from a boat looking at the Miami skyline — the same image Hugh searches for again at his lowest point.
